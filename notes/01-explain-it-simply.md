# The whole thing, explained simply

No equations. This is the version for a friend, a parent, or the first three minutes of a talk.

## A fact about stars

The Sun is not equally bright all over. The middle of the disc is brighter than the edge, and you can see this in almost any photograph of it.

The reason is that the Sun has no solid surface. It is a ball of hot gas that fades out gradually. When you look at the middle, you are looking straight down into it, and you see deep, where it is hotter and brighter. When you look near the edge, your view goes in at a slant and stops higher up, where the gas is cooler and dimmer. This is called limb darkening. Every star does it.

## Why people outside solar physics care

Nearly every planet we know of around another star was found by watching the star dim slightly as the planet crossed in front of it. The depth and shape of that dip tell us how big the planet is.

But the dip does not depend only on the planet. When the planet crosses the bright middle of the star it blocks a lot of light. When it crosses the dim edge it blocks less. So to measure the planet you have to know how the star fades from the middle to the edge.

For every star except one, you cannot look and see, because the star is a single point of light. You have to calculate it from a computer model of the star's atmosphere and trust the model.

## The problem, and the proposed answer

In 2024 a group at the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research published a paper in Nature Astronomy showing the models are wrong in a consistent direction. Real stars, measured through transits by the Kepler and TESS telescopes, are slightly brighter near their edges than the models predict. Not by much, but always the same way.

Then they explained it. Every model before theirs assumed the star had no magnetic field. Real stars are covered in tiny magnetic patches, far too small to see individually, and those patches let you see a little deeper into the gas. Put magnetic fields into the simulation and the disagreement goes away.

If it holds, it improves the measured size of every known planet, which is why it was published where it was.

## What I looked at

A result only means what it claims if the boring explanations have been killed first. So I went through them, and then I looked at how strong the remaining evidence actually is.

The disagreement is measured through two numbers, not one. Think of them as two different ways of asking how fast the star fades towards its edge: one measures the fade three quarters of the way out, the other compares that against the fade almost at the edge. The Göttingen paper's headline is that magnetism explains **both** at once, which is a much stronger claim than explaining one.

The first number holds up under everything I could throw at it. The second does not, and the evidence for that is sitting in the paper the measurements came from.

The astronomer who made those measurements, Pierre Maxted, had also measured sixteen of the same stars in an earlier study. When he compared the two sets of results he found the first number was unchanged, and the second had shifted by an amount as large as the effect itself, purely because he had processed the data differently the second time. He says so plainly in his paper: the first quantity is reliable, the second one may be off by about that much depending on how you do the analysis. The 2024 paper does not mention this.

There is a second sign of the same thing. Maxted also looked up what a magnetic field of the expected strength should do, from an earlier simulation. It predicted the first number almost exactly, and it predicted less than half of the second. He notes that too.

So the case for magnetism appears to rest on one measurement rather than two. That is still a real case, and I could not break it. It is just a narrower one than the paper presents.

## What else I checked, and what survived

Before concluding that, I tried to kill the first number with the boring explanations, and failed twice, which is a result in the Göttingen group's favour.

**Could the stars' recorded temperatures or compositions be wrong?** That would shift the prediction with no magnetism involved. It would take an error about nine times larger than the stated uncertainties, pointing the same way for every star in two separate surveys. Not believable.

**Could the planet just be crossing bright patches?** Stars have bright magnetic blotches called faculae. If the planet's path ran across a lot of them the star would look brighter along that path. This objection was raised in print, but never with a number. So I attached one. Using the newest measurements of how bright solar faculae are, the planet's path would have to be between a quarter and seventy per cent covered in them. These are calm stars chosen for being well behaved. Not possible.

**Could the calculation be borrowing from the wrong star?** The magnetic correction is worked out by simulating the Sun, and then applied to these stars on the grounds that they are close enough to solar. On average they are 583 degrees hotter than the Sun and noticeably richer in heavy elements. Staying inside one model and one telescope, so that nothing else can be blamed, moving across that gap changes the fading pattern by about two and a half times the effect being explained. This does not undo their result, because the comparison against the data is done star by star. It does mean that carrying a solar answer across that distance is a step with an unstated error on it, and their own follow-up paper says the magnetic effect grows in exactly that direction.

## The thing that does not go away

The disagreement is measured by subtracting one particular computer model. There are nine published models of this kind, and the same observations had already been compared against all nine. Lining them up, the effect being explained is about six units and the spread between the nine models is fifty-seven. One of the nine gives the opposite sign.

Nothing in the planet data tells you which model to pick, and the size of the magnetic effect depends entirely on that choice.

## The way to pin it down

There is exactly one star where you do not need a model, because you can photograph the disc and simply measure how it fades from middle to edge. It is the Sun.

So the test is straightforward. Take all nine models. Ask each one what the Sun should look like, using the Sun's temperature and composition, which we know precisely. Compare against the measured Sun. The model that gets the Sun right is the one you should be using for the other stars, and once you have chosen it, the size of the magnetic effect follows.

I checked whether the measurement of the Sun is good enough for this. There are two independent measurements of the solar profile, one from 1977 and one from 1994. They agree with each other about fifty times better than the models disagree with each other. So yes, comfortably good enough.

Two of the nine have been checked against the Sun already, including the one the 2024 result uses, and both come out well. The other seven have not, and it is the choice among all of them that sets the size of the magnetic effect. The reason nobody has finished the job is mundane. The solar measurements exist at individual colours, the models are tabulated averaged over a telescope's whole colour range, and somebody has to convert between the two. The Göttingen group did that conversion once, for their own model. I measured how much the conversion is worth, and it is about six times the size of the effect being argued about, so it is the whole job rather than a detail on the end of it.

## The honest summary

I did not discover anything. I did the unglamorous job of asking what else could have produced somebody else's published effect.

Two of the alternative explanations died, with numbers, which strengthens their case. One survives and is bigger than the effect. And half of their headline claim turns out to rest on a quantity that the paper supplying it says is unreliable at exactly that level, which nobody had connected.

None of that makes them wrong. It makes the case narrower than stated, and it points at one short piece of work that would settle the remaining doubt. That work needs the Sun, and it needs somebody who knows how to measure a solar image very carefully. Which is a solar physics job, applied to an exoplanet problem, using data that has been public for thirty years.
